Meghdoot: how our climate digital twin works

Team Aflatoon · Yugaantar DevForge 2026, Track 2 · internal explainer for the desk review

Meghdoot (the "Multiverse of Monsoon" digital twin) is named after Kalidasa's Meghadūta, in which a monsoon cloud carries a message across India. Our model does something similar: a change made in one place is carried onward at a bounded speed. Repo: github.com/Team-Aflatoon/Meghdoot · one-page brief: docs/BRIEF.pdf · all experiment numbers: docs/EXPERIMENTS.md.

Read it top to bottom once. Then use the Q&A drill to rehearse. Every number lives in the Results section; elsewhere numbers are pulled from there and look like this: 0.000. If a number changes, edit only the Results table.

1. The pitch

30-second version

"We built a fast, offline climate digital twin of India: a small neural network that steps the weather forward one day at a time on the official 32 × 36 grid. What makes it different: each daily step can only see a few neighbouring cells, so a shock cannot teleport. It starts where you apply it and spreads at a bounded speed. That is a property of the architecture, and we have a bit-exact test for it. A zero shock gives exactly the same output. In held-out evaluation on 2020–2021 (the shipped model never saw those years), scored with the kit's own scoring code, a 5-member ensemble beats the better of persistence and climatology (dense skill over 144 windows)."

1-minute version

  1. What it is. A climate digital twin: give it 3 days of real weather, it rolls out the next N days (Timeline Prime). Change the starting state in one region, it shows the alternate future (the Multiverse) and how the change spreads.
  2. How. A residual CNN ("StencilNet") that works in anomaly space (departure from the normal weather for that day of the year) and predicts only tomorrow's change. Rain is never negative and temperature is clamped to valid values after every step. Five trained networks are averaged (the "ens5" ensemble), with nothing applied after the average except a NaN / range guard. We tried shrinking long-range anomalies towards the normal weather (anomaly damping) and retraining on all years; neither is shipped.
  3. USP. Causality by construction. The network has only 3×3 and 1×1 convolutions: no pooling, no attention, no global layers. So information moves at most a fixed number of cells per simulated day (6 for the three plain members, 8 for the two advection members). The judge calls anything faster than 8 cells/day "teleportation". Our test test_no_teleport_by_construction checks that cells beyond that reach are bit-for-bit unchanged.
  4. Proof. Held-out 2020–2021 (the shipped model), the kit's own scoring.py: dense mean skill (persistence: ), heavy-rain CSI skill (dense), causality /15 in all 6 named windows. The shipped members were trained on 2005–2019 only, so every held-out number here belongs to the shipped model and the 2021 dashboard scenarios are genuinely unseen by it (see Results). Runs offline in the judge's container in per 10-day call.
  5. Impact. A "Heat and Kharif water-stress co-pilot" in the dashboard turns Prime vs Multiverse into per-region heat-stress days, rain change, dry days and a one-line advisory. The dashboard also draws calibrated chance-of-heavy-rain and chance-of-heat maps (risk maps), which are not part of the judged contract.

2. The problem in plain words

What is a climate digital twin?

A computer copy of the atmosphere over India that you can run forward in time, and that you can poke ("what if the Bay of Bengal were 3 °C warmer?") to see what would happen.

A learned simulator f that maps the last 3 daily states to the next one. Applied repeatedly (an autoregressive rollout), it produces a 10-day forecast. Applied to a perturbed initial state, it produces a counterfactual forecast.

Timeline Prime

The normal forecast: real starting days, no change. "What will happen."

Multiverse

The same start with a shock added to the last input day (for example warm the Bay of Bengal by 3 K). "What would happen if." The interesting output is the difference Multiverse − Prime: where it starts, how fast it spreads, whether it stays sane.

The data and the grid

  • 17 years of daily weather, 2005–2021 (6,209 days) in india_climate_train.nc, derived from ERA5 (each cell is the mean of the 4 surrounding 1° ERA5 points).
  • Grid: 32 × 36 cells of 1°. Cell centres from latitude 6.5° to 37.5° N (south to north, 32 rows) and longitude 65.5° to 100.5° E (west to east, 36 columns). One cell is roughly 111 km tall.
  • Two static fields: land-sea mask and orography (terrain height). The API never sends them; we ship them in weights/static.npy.
  • stats.json: mean and standard deviation per channel, for normalising inside the model.

What the judge does (8 blind calls)

  • 5 real windows with hidden dates after 2023-01-10 (they are never sent, only grids and day of year).
  • 3 shocks on one real window:
    • warm_ocean: +3.0 K on t2m and +15% of the current tcwv over the Bay of Bengal box (lat 8–20, lon 82–94).
    • monsoon_stall: −40% of the current u10 and v10 over the Arabian Sea box (lat 8–20, lon 65–75).
    • null: an all-zero perturbation; must equal the unperturbed call.
  • Runtime: CPU only, 4 cores, 6 GB RAM, no internet, repo read-only, each 10-step call under 5.0 s, /health ready within 120 s.

The causality checks, exactly

The judge computes the response Δ = shocked rollout − base rollout. "Energy" per cell per day = sum over channels of (Δ / std)², using the channel std from stats.json.

  1. Non-zero: somewhere, some channel moves by at least 1e-3 std. (A model that ignores shocks gets 0 of 15.)
  2. Localised: on day 1, at least 60% of the response energy is within 3 cells of the shock box (the box grown by 3 cells with 4-neighbour steps).
  3. Propagates: two conditions together. (a) Grows outward: the mean energy outside the 3-cell neighbourhood over days 4–8 is more than 1.5 × the outside energy on day 1, and more than 2% of the peak daily total energy. (b) No teleport: on every day k (1-based), less than 5% of the energy lies further than 3 + 8·k cells (Euclidean distance) from the box. 8 cells/day is about 900 km/day, "faster than any atmospheric signal worth the name".
  4. Stable: the response never exceeds 5 std anywhere.

Scoring per shock: no response → 0. Response but not localised → only the "non-zero" quarter. Localised → 2 quarters plus one each for propagates and stable. Null shock: max |a − b| / std ≤ 1e-3 or the causality total is halved.

The qualifying gate (needed for the Top 10)

  • Valid output on all 8 calls: correct shape, no NaN/Inf, rain ≥ 0 (tolerance −1e-6), t2m between −40 and +60 °C (233.15–333.15 K).
  • Beat persistence at day 4.
  • A non-zero, localised response to at least one shock.

A failed or invalid window scores 0 for that window; it is never skipped.

4. Our architecture, step by step

Diagram

Figure 1. One daily step of one member, the loop, and the ensemble. Source: model/net.py, model/simulator.py.

The steps

  1. Inputs. 3 days × 6 channels × 32 × 36 in physical units, plus doy_sincos of the last day. If a perturbation is sent, it is added to the last input day first (model/simulator.py). NaN/Inf input cells (which the contract forbids) are filled from that day's climatology as a safety net.
  2. Day of year. doy_index_from_sincos turns [sin, cos] back into a slot 0–364 with atan2. Training uses the same function, so the slot is identical in training and serving. No date ever enters the server.
  3. Anomaly normalisation. For each input day: anomaly = (x − climatology[slot]) / std. The climatology is a 365-day array (weights/climatology.npy, shape 365 × 6 × 32 × 36), the mean of each calendar day over the training years of that weight set only (2005–2019), smoothed with a 15-day circular running mean (train/prepare.py). std comes from stats.json.
  4. Features (step_features, 29 channels): the 3 anomalies (18), the normalised climatology of the target day (6), land-sea mask and orography in km (2), sin/cos of the target day (2), and latitude as (lat − 22)/10 (1).
  5. StencilNet. Stem: 3×3 conv to 96 channels + GELU. Then residual blocks: h + Conv1×1(GELU(Conv3×3(h))), 5 blocks for the plain members, 4 for the advection member. Head: 1×1 conv to 6 channels, initialised to zero, so an untrained net predicts "no change in anomaly" (anomaly persistence). All 3×3 convs use replicate padding (edge cells are copied outward, rather than padding with zeros that would look like a fake signal).
  6. Predict the change. The net outputs Δ, and a_next = a_today + Δ (residual learning).
  7. Add back the climatology. x_next = climatology[next day] + a_next × std. When the net has nothing to say, the forecast is "today's anomaly on top of the normal weather for tomorrow", never a runaway.
  8. Physical gate. Rain through ReLU (≥ 0), t2m clamped to 234.15–332.15 K. The gated state is what the next step sees.
  9. Autoregressive loop. The new day is appended; the oldest drops off. Repeat for N days (server allows 1–30).
  10. Ensemble. The 5 members each do a full rollout and the rollouts are averaged.
  11. No damping. The averaged rollout is returned as it is. We tested shrinking anomalies towards climatology by a lead-dependent factor (train/fit_damping.py), but it is not applied by the server and there is no weights/damping.npy. See the glossary and the "tried and rejected" card in section 5.
  12. Final guard. Any non-finite value falls back to that day's climatology, then t2m and rain are clamped again.

Reach per step (why it cannot teleport)

A 3×3 convolution lets a cell see its 8 neighbours: reach grows by 1 cell. A 1×1 convolution and GELU are per-cell: reach +0.

The semi-Lagrangian advection members (adv1, adv1s1)

Weather patterns get blown along by the wind. Instead of making the CNN learn "move things downwind" from scratch, this member first physically shifts today's anomaly field along the wind, then the CNN corrects what is left.

For each cell, the displacement in cells per day is dx = α · u10 · (86400 / 111 000) / cos(lat), dy = α · v10 · 86400 / 111 000, where α is a learned scalar (starts at 0.5). Displacements are capped smoothly with cap · tanh(d / cap), cap = 2 cells/day. Each cell takes the value found at its upstream point (i − dy, j − dx) (that is "semi-Lagrangian": look back along the wind), read with bilinear interpolation (torch.nn.functional.grid_sample, border padding). The carried anomaly is fed to the CNN as 6 extra input channels, and a_next = carried + CNN(...).

  • Why α is learned: the 10 m wind is not the wind that actually steers weather systems (that is higher up); α lets training find the right scale.
  • Why the tanh cap: a speed limit, like a CFL condition. Without it a strong wind could move a signal arbitrarily far in one step and break the reach guarantee.
  • Why 4 blocks, not 5: reach = 2 (cap) + 1 (bilinear reads the next cell) + 1 (stem) + 4 (blocks) = 8, exactly the judge's 8 cells/day. With 5 blocks it would be 9. tests/test_model.py::test_reach_within_judge_speed_limit asserts reach ≤ 8.
  • Honest evidence: on its own, adv1 was "no clear win" (it bundled two changes, 1 run), and its twin adv1s1 sits in the same range as the plain members. They earn their place by adding diversity to the ensemble; see Results.

Calibrated risk maps (dashboard only, /risk)

Besides the judged /simulate, the server has a dashboard-only /risk endpoint (not part of the judged contract). It answers "what is the chance of heavy rain / heat in this cell on day k?" using the 5 members as a small ensemble.

  1. Raw share. For each cell and day, p_raw = the share of the 5 members whose forecast is above the threshold: heavy rain > 10 mm/day, heat = daily-mean t2m ≥ 34 °C. With 5 members the values are 0, 0.2, 0.4 … 1.
  2. Blend with history. p_cal = w[k]·p_raw + (1 − w[k])·base[month, cell], where base is the historical monthly frequency of the event in the training years (before 2020) and w[k] is a per-lead weight fitted by least squares out of sample (train/fit_risk.py; stored in weights/risk.npz).
  3. Why the blend. Five members trained on the same data are similar, so they agree more often than the truth justifies. Raw shares are over-confident: they say "100%" or "0%" too readily. Blending with the base rate pulls them back towards honest odds, and the weight shrinks as the lead grows (w_rain 0.59 → 0.03 and w_heat 0.83 → 0.33 over 10 days), so at long leads the map is mostly "normal odds for the month" with a small model signal.
  4. Result. Brier skill against normal odds on held-out 2021 is positive at every lead for both events (Results). The raw shares were negative for heavy rain after day 1.

Impact. The Multiverse can be shown as a change in probability (for example "the chance of heavy rain drops under Monsoon stall"), which is easier for a decision-maker to act on than a single deterministic map. Honest limits: the skill beyond day 4 is small (heavy rain +0.004 at day 8), and 5 members cannot represent the real spread of weather.

5. Why it works: design decisions

Each row: the problem, what we did, and how we know.

No drift over long rollouts

Problem
Small errors compound when a model feeds on its own output; forecasts wander off or explode.
Fix
(1) Predict the change of the anomaly, not the full state. (2) Work relative to climatology, so "no information" means "normal weather for that day". (3) Train on unrolled rollouts of 1 → 2 → 4 → 6 days, so the model is penalised for its own compounding errors. (4) Physical gate every step.
Evidence
test_extreme_inputs_stay_valid: +25 K heat, ×20 rain and ×5 wind for 30 days stays finite and valid. Causality scored 13.12/15 in all 6 named ens5 windows, and the earlier ens3 was stable on all 12 shocks (Results).

No blur

Problem
Models trained on squared error tend to forecast a smooth average field, which loses detail at day 8.
Fix
Anomaly + residual design keeps today's structure as the starting point; we measure the kit's day-8 variance ratio for every model.
Evidence
Anti-blur scored 10/10 on the 144-window mean on our local judge (derivation in Results). Caveat: on a single random draw of 5 windows there is about a chance of scoring 8.33 instead of 10, because rain on a dry day can push the day-8 variance ratio past 2.5 (expected score about ). Note: the original plan included a variance loss term; we did not need it and it is not in the code.

No teleport, by construction

Problem
The judge penalises a response far from the shock (faster than 8 cells/day).
Fix
Only 3×3 and 1×1 convolutions plus per-cell operations. No pooling, no attention, no BatchNorm/LayerNorm over space, no global averages: anything global would let one cell affect every cell instantly.
Evidence
test_no_teleport_by_construction: a 1-cell shock; every cell beyond radius × day (square distance) is checked to be exactly unchanged (max diff == 0.0), and the shock does produce a response.

Null shock identical

Problem
If zero shock gives a slightly different answer, half the causality points go.
Fix
Deterministic CPU inference: eval mode, no dropout or sampling, fixed 4 threads, same member order. Adding zeros leaves the floats unchanged.
Evidence
test_null_shock_is_identical asserts max |a − b| == 0.0; test_deterministic asserts two identical calls are equal. The dashboard shows "Multiverse ≡ Prime" for the zero preset.

Rain never negative, t2m valid

Problem
Negative rain or out-of-range temperature fails the validity gate (no Top 10).
Fix
ReLU on rain and a t2m clamp inside every step (so the next step also sees a valid state), plus a final guard in the simulator (NaN → climatology, clamp again).
Evidence
test_shape_and_validity_gate, test_extreme_inputs_stay_valid; no invalid windows on the local judge.

Heavy rain not smoothed away

Problem
Heavy-rain cells are rare, so a plain loss ignores them and CSI suffers.
Fix
Cells with true rain > 10 mm/day (the CSI threshold) get 4× loss weight (1 + 3).
Evidence
Positive CSI skill on both window sets (Results). We did not run an ablation without the weight; say so if asked.

Ensemble of 5

Problem
A single model's errors include random, seed-dependent parts, and occasional rain spikes blew the 5-std stability limit.
Fix
Average 5 independently trained members: 3 plain (different seeds) and 2 structurally different (wind advection, different seeds).
Why it helps
RMSE punishes big errors. Members make partly independent errors, and averaging cancels some of them, so the mean is closer to the truth on average. It also averages out a spike that only one member produces.
Evidence
ens2 beat its best member, ens3 beat ens2, and ens5 beat ens3 on dense skill; stability went from failing some shocks (v1) to stable on all ens3 shocks (Results). Averaging is deterministic, so the null shock is still exact. The anti-blur check still passes with the average.

Tried and rejected: anomaly damping, and retraining on all years

Idea 1: damping
At long leads the network's anomalies are over-confident. out_k = clim_k + λ[k,c] · (out_k − clim_k) shrinks each forecast anomaly towards the normal weather by a factor λ in [0, 1] that depends on lead day k and channel c (λ fitted by least squares on 2020, rain left at 1). Out of sample on 2021 (71 dense windows) it gained only +0.001 pooled and +0.002 per-lead skill (table in Results).
Idea 2: all-years retrain
Retrain the same 5 recipes on 2005–2021 for shipping. No held-out year would remain, so any forecast benefit is unmeasurable.
Why neither ships
A seasonal causality sweep (72 windows, both official shocks) showed a cost: damping −0.16 /15 (13.10 → 12.94), all-years retrain −0.34 (13.10 → 12.76), both together 12.60. A tiny measurable gain against a real causality loss. Caveat: one sweep each over 72 correlated windows, so differences of about 0.3 may be partly noise; we chose the variant whose every number is measured out of sample.
Status
The damping code is kept as an option but is off; the server applies no damping and there is no damping.npy.

Calibrated risk maps

Problem
A deterministic map says "rain here" or not. Decision-makers want "how likely", and the 5 raw members over-state their confidence.
Fix
Share of the 5 members above the threshold, blended with the historical monthly frequency using per-lead weights fitted out of sample (train/fit_risk.py, dashboard-only /risk endpoint). See the architecture note.
Evidence
Brier skill vs the base rate on held-out 2021 (fit on 2020): heavy rain / / and heat / / at days 1 / 4 / 8. Before calibration, heavy rain was negative at days 4 and 8 (−0.618, −0.923). Test: test_risk_endpoint_probabilities.

Honest validation

Problem
Scoring on training years, or with our own re-implementation of the rules, would flatter us.
Fix
Train the 5 members on 2005–2019 only and test on held-out 2020–2021. That ens5 is exactly what ships, so every held-out number is a number of the shipped model, and the 2021 dashboard scenarios are genuinely unseen by it. train/evaluate.py imports the kit's own functions (rmse_lat, skill_vs_best_ref, csi, csi_skill, forecast_points, variance_ratio, anti_blur_points, make_shock, causality_checks, causality_points, null_consistent, validity). Climatology reference = the kit's climatology over training years.
Two window sets
named = 10 windows (5 per year) matching the hidden case types: winter western disturbance 25 Jan, heatwave 15 May, onset 5 Jun, heavy rain 20 Jul, cyclone 20 Oct. dense = every 5th day of 2020–2021 = 144 windows. Dense is more trustworthy: 144 samples instead of 10, so one lucky or unlucky window barely moves the mean (seed-to-seed spread on named alone was about ±0.02 SS).
Assumptions
We assume the judge's per-window SS is the mean over channels of SS on leads 1/4/8 stacked. Causality is checked on the named heavy-rain, onset and cyclone windows (6 windows × 2 shocks = 12 shocks), and separately over 72 windows across all seasons (train/causality_sweep.py). The skill averaging across leads is itself an assumption: see How is skill averaged?

7. Concepts and glossary

General definitions are standard textbook meanings; anything specific to our model points at the code.

Weather and climate

Anomaly
How far today is from normal for this day of the year. Example: Delhi normally 30 °C on 15 May; today 34 °C → anomaly +4 °C. We divide by the channel std, so the anomaly is in "standard deviations".
Climatology
The long-term average weather for each calendar day. Ours: mean over the training years per day of year (2005–2019), smoothed over 15 days, 365 slots (weights/climatology.npy). It is a single "typical year", which the rules allow (multi-year archives are not).
Persistence baseline
"Tomorrow (and every later day) equals today." Hard to beat at day 1, poor at day 8.
Climatology baseline
"Every forecast day equals the normal for that date." Poor at day 1, decent at day 8. The judge compares us to the better of the two.
ERA5
ECMWF's global reanalysis of the atmosphere, hourly, from 1940 on. Our dataset is ERA5 averaged to 1° and to daily values.
Reanalysis
A best-estimate reconstruction of past weather made by combining observations with a weather model. It is "the truth" for training here, though not perfect.
IMD
India Meteorological Department, the official forecaster. We borrow its thresholds (2.5 mm rainy day) and note where we differ (heatwave uses daily max).
Kharif
The monsoon-season crop cycle (sown with the monsoon rains, e.g. rice). Rain deficit at sowing is what our water-stress advisory flags.
Western disturbance
A storm system arriving from the west (Mediterranean side) that brings winter rain and snow to north India. One of the hidden test case types (our named window: 25 Jan).
Monsoon onset
The start of the summer monsoon rains, marked over Kerala around early June (our named window: 5 Jun).
Bay of Bengal / Arabian Sea branches
The summer monsoon flow reaches India in two arms: one over the Arabian Sea to the west coast, one over the Bay of Bengal to the east and northeast. The judge's two shock boxes sit on these: warm_ocean on the Bay, monsoon_stall on the Arabian Sea.
t2m, tp, u10, v10, msl, tcwv
See the table in section 2. u10/v10 are wind components at 10 m: u is east-west, v is north-south. u10 = −5, v10 = 0 means a 5 m/s wind blowing towards the west. msl: low values mark depressions and cyclones. tcwv: the water available to rain out.
Cyclone core, orographic rain
A cyclone's intense centre is tens of km across; orographic rain is rain forced by air rising over mountains (Western Ghats). Both are smaller than one 1° cell.

Metrics

RMSE
Root mean squared error: square the errors, average, take the square root. Big errors count extra. Errors 1, 1, 4 → √((1+1+16)/3) ≈ 2.45 (plain average would be 2).
Latitude weighting
Grid cells further north are physically smaller (meridians converge), so each row's error is weighted by cos(latitude), normalised to mean 1. At 37.5° N the weight is about 0.79 of that at the equator before normalising.
Skill score (SS)
1 − RMSE_model / RMSE_reference. 0 = as good as the reference, 1 = perfect, negative = worse. Reference RMSE 2.0, ours 1.8 → SS = 0.10.
CSI (critical success index)
For a yes/no event (rain > 10 mm/day): hits / (hits + misses + false alarms). Correct "no" cells do not count. 8 hits, 6 misses, 6 false alarms → 8/20 = 0.40. CSI skill compares this to the better baseline: (CSI − ref)/(1 − ref).
Brier score
The squared error of a probability forecast of a yes/no event: average of (p − o)², where o is 1 if the event happened and 0 if not. Lower is better. Forecasting 0.8 and it happens: (0.8 − 1)² = 0.04; forecasting 0.8 and it does not: 0.64.
Brier skill score (BSS)
1 − Brier_model / Brier_reference, where the reference is "always forecast the base rate". 0 = no better than normal odds, 1 = perfect, negative = worse than normal odds. Our calibrated heat probability scores +0.58 at day 1 on 2021.
Calibration
A probability forecast is calibrated if events forecast at 30% really happen about 30% of the time. We calibrate by blending the raw member share with the historical frequency, with weights fitted on held-out data.
Base rate (climatological frequency)
How often the event happens in the historical record for that month and place. It is the "normal odds" reference for the Brier skill score and the anchor for our blend.
Over-confidence (under-dispersion)
An ensemble whose members agree more often than the truth justifies: the spread is too small, so probabilities from member counts are too close to 0 or 1. Five members trained on the same data are similar, so ours are over-confident until calibrated. If all 5 members say "heavy rain" but only 40% of such days see it, the raw 100% is over-confident.
Variance ratio (anti-blur)
How "contrasty" our day-8 map is compared with the real one: spatial variance ours / real. 1.0 = same amount of structure; 0.2 = washed out.

Model and training

Convolution
A small filter slid over the grid; each output cell is a weighted sum of a small neighbourhood. 3×3 = the cell and its 8 neighbours; 1×1 = only the cell itself (mixes channels).
Receptive field
The set of input cells that can influence one output cell. Ours grows by 1 cell per 3×3 conv: 6 cells per day for the 3 plain members, 8 for the 2 advection members.
Replicate padding
At the map border, the conv needs cells outside the map; we copy the edge values outward rather than inventing zeros.
Residual connection
A layer outputs input + correction instead of a brand-new value. Easier to train and stable. We use it twice: inside blocks, and for the whole step (predict the change).
GELU
A smooth activation function: roughly "pass positives, squash negatives", but without ReLU's sharp kink.
ReLU
max(0, x). We use it as the rain gate, which makes negative rain impossible.
Zero-init head
The final layer starts with all weights zero, so before training the model predicts "anomaly unchanged". Training starts from a sane forecast instead of noise.
Normalisation
Rescaling inputs to similar ranges (here: subtract climatology, divide by std) so pressure (≈ 100 000 Pa) and rain (≈ 4 mm) count equally. Note: we use NO normalisation layers that average over space (BatchNorm/GroupNorm/LayerNorm), because those would mix information across the whole map.
Autoregressive rollout
Predict day 1, feed it back as input, predict day 2, and so on. Errors can compound, which is why we train on rollouts.
Teacher forcing vs unrolled training
Teacher forcing: train only to predict 1 day from true inputs. Unrolled: let the model run several days on its own predictions and backpropagate the error of every day. Our first 12 epochs are the 1-day phase; the rest are unrolled.
Curriculum
Teach easy first: 1-day steps, then 2, 4, 6-day rollouts (--schedule 1:12,2:6,4:6,6:6).
Ensemble
Several models whose outputs are averaged. Ours: 5 members (3 plain, 2 advection, different seeds), same inputs, averaged rollouts.
Anomaly damping (tried, not shipped)
Shrinking over-confident long-range anomalies back towards the average: out_k = clim_k + λ[k,c] · (out_k − clim_k), λ between 0 and 1, fitted per lead day and per variable by least squares on years the model did not train on. λ = 1 means "leave it alone", λ = 0 means "just say the normal weather". Example: at day 8 the model says t2m is 5 K above normal; λ = 0.82 turns that into 4.1 K above normal. We tested it (+0.001 pooled skill, −0.16 causality) and the server does not apply it.
Pooled vs per-lead skill
Pooled: put leads 1, 4 and 8 into one RMSE per channel, then compute SS once. Per-lead: compute SS separately for each lead, then average. Per-lead gives day 8 (where we only match climatology) an equal vote, so it is harsher. The contract does not say which; see How is skill averaged?
Semi-Lagrangian advection
Moving a field along the wind by asking, for each cell, "where did the air here come from?" and reading the value there (with interpolation). Stable even for big time steps.
CFL-like speed limit
From numerical weather models: information must not travel more than a set number of cells per step. Ours: tanh cap of 2 cells/day in advection; overall reach ≤ 8 cells/day.
Bilinear interpolation / grid_sample
Reading a value between grid points by blending the 4 nearest cells. PyTorch's grid_sample does this for a whole field at once, and it is differentiable.
Overfitting / held-out validation
Overfitting = memorising the training years. To detect it, score on years the model never saw (2020–2021; the shipped weights never saw them, so those scores are honest for the shipped model).
AdamW
A standard optimiser (Adam) with weight decay applied separately (the "W"), which gently pulls weights towards zero to reduce overfitting.
Cosine LR schedule
The learning rate starts at 1e-3 and glides down to ~0 along a half cosine over all 30 epochs.
Gradient clipping
If the gradient is huge (common in long rollouts), rescale it to norm 1.0 so one bad batch cannot wreck the weights.
Epoch
One pass over all training samples.
Deterministic inference
Same input → bit-identical output, every time. Needed for the null shock.
Std (σ)
Standard deviation, the typical size of variation. Channel std from stats.json: t2m 13.46 K, tp 7.55 mm/day, u10 2.90 m/s, v10 2.43 m/s, msl 619 Pa, tcwv 20.49 kg/m².
Chebyshev vs Euclidean distance
Chebyshev = max(|Δrow|, |Δcol|) (a square); Euclidean = straight-line distance (a circle). Our construction bounds Chebyshev reach; the judge measures Euclidean.

10. Judge Q&A drill

Click to reveal. Answer in the first sentence, then give one piece of evidence.

Design

1. In one sentence, what is your model?

A residual CNN that steps India's weather forward one day at a time in anomaly space, with a small fixed receptive field so shocks cannot jump; five of them averaged. A calibrated probability layer on top gives chance-of-heavy-rain and chance-of-heat maps for the dashboard.

2. Why not a U-Net or a transformer?

Both are built to mix information across the whole map: a U-Net downsamples (pooling), so one step can reach far; attention connects every cell to every cell. That breaks the causality the judge tests for: a shock would affect India in one step. Our stencil makes the speed limit a guarantee, not a hope. It is also small and fast on CPU ( per 10-day call). Trade-off: less long-range context per step, which we accept.

3. How do you know it doesn't teleport?

Two levels. By construction: only 3×3 and 1×1 convolutions and per-cell operations, so each step can move information at most 6 cells (8 for the advection members). By test: test_no_teleport_by_construction puts a shock in one cell and checks that every cell beyond reach × day is exactly unchanged, difference 0.0, not just small. And by the judge's own causality_checks run locally: no shock flagged for teleporting. One subtlety, if pushed: we bound distance in squares, the judge measures circles; the far diagonal corner is theoretical and carries negligible energy.

4. Why anomalies instead of raw values?

Most of the raw signal is the seasonal cycle, which we already know from the calendar. Subtracting climatology leaves the part that is actually hard: today's departure from normal. It also gives a safe default: if the network is unsure, the forecast falls back to "normal weather plus today's anomaly", and long rollouts stay near climatology instead of drifting. And it puts all 6 variables on the same scale.

5. Why predict the change rather than the next state?

Day-to-day changes are small, so learning a correction is easier and more stable than re-creating the whole map. With a zero-initialised head, the untrained model already gives a reasonable forecast (anomaly persistence).

6. Why does the zero shock give exactly zero?

The server adds the perturbation to the last input day; adding zeros leaves every float identical. Inference is deterministic on CPU: eval mode, no dropout, no random sampling, fixed thread count, members always in the same order. Same input bits → same output bits. test_null_shock_is_identical asserts a maximum difference of exactly 0.0. The judge only needs 1e-3 std.

7. Why doesn't it explode over 10 (or 30) days?

Four reasons: residual updates in anomaly space; the climatology anchor; training on unrolled 2, 4 and 6-day rollouts on its own predictions, so it learned not to amplify its own errors; and a physical gate every step. Evidence: the 3-member ensemble was stable on local shocks, ens5 scores causality /15 in all 6 named windows, and a test that runs absurd inputs (+25 K, ×20 rain, ×5 wind) for 30 days and stays finite and valid.

8. What happens after 30 days?

The server accepts 1 to 30 steps (more returns a 422 error, by our choice). Physically, after a couple of weeks any forecast loses day-to-day skill; by design ours relaxes towards climatology rather than drifting or exploding. We do not claim skill there; the judge scores days 1, 4, 8.

9. Why doesn't it blur?

Today's structure is the starting point (residual in anomaly space) instead of being re-generated, and the rain weighting keeps extremes in the loss. We measure it with the kit's own variance-ratio function: full anti-blur points on our local judge. Even the 5-member average keeps enough variance on the 144-window mean (10/10). Honest caveat: on a random draw of just 5 windows there is about a chance of scoring 8.33 instead of 10, because rain on a dry day can push the variance ratio past 2.5.

10. Why an ensemble, and doesn't averaging blur?

Each trained network makes partly random errors; averaging cancels some of them, which lowers RMSE. Logged: the 2-member ensemble beat its best member by dense SS, and ens5 beats the best single member by . It also fixed stability: a single model had rain spikes above 5 std on shocks; the 3-member average had none. Yes, averaging smooths a little, but the day-8 variance stays above the 40% line on our local judge. Averaging is deterministic, so the null shock stays exact.

11. What is the advection member, and why only 4 blocks?

Before the CNN, it shifts today's anomaly along the 10 m wind (semi-Lagrangian, bilinear), with a learned scale and a smooth cap of 2 cells per day. That costs 3 cells of reach (2 + 1 for interpolation), so with stem + 4 blocks the total is exactly 8, the judge's limit. Alone it was no clear win; it helps the ensemble by being different.

12. Why replicate padding?

Zero padding would make the map edge look like a strong negative anomaly and inject fake signals. Copying the edge values is neutral, and it never brings in information from further than one cell.

Validation and numbers

13. How did you validate?

Model selection used members trained on 2005–2019 only, scored on held-out 2020–2021 with the kit's own scoring functions imported directly: RMSE skill, CSI, anti-blur, causality with the official make_shock, null consistency, validity. Two window sets: 10 "named" windows matching the hidden case types, and 144 "dense" windows (every 5th day), which we trust more. The climatology reference is also built from training years only. The shipped model is exactly these 5 members (no retrain, no damping), so every held-out number is a number of the shipped model. The risk-map weights were fitted on 2020 and checked on 2021 (out of sample).

14. What is your skill score?

Dense, 144 held-out windows (the shipped model): mean SS against the better of persistence and climatology. Named, 10 windows: . Full marks need 0.12, so on the dense estimate we expect roughly of 30, if the judge pools leads. See question 32 for the other reading.

15. What is your CSI and why is it what it is?

CSI skill over the better baseline: dense, named; full marks at 0.10. It is positive because heavy-rain cells get 4× weight in the loss. It is not higher because heavy rain is patchy and sub-grid at 1°, and at day 8 the exact location is close to unpredictable.

16. Why is rain skill the weakest?

Rain is intermittent (zero most places, huge in a few), its daily value is a sum, not a mean, it depends on processes smaller than a 1° cell (convection, mountains), and it errs in both position and amount, which RMSE double-penalises. Persistence and climatology are also comparatively good references for rain at some leads. Temperature, pressure and water vapour are smoother and easier.

17. Named vs dense: why two numbers?

Named (10 windows) mirrors the hidden test's case types, but 10 samples are noisy: two seeds of the same recipe differed by about ±0.02. Dense (144 windows) gives a stable mean. We choose models on dense.

18. Do you beat persistence at day 4 (the gate)?

Yes, our local judge checks the gate on every evaluation and ens5 passes it: mean SS against persistence at day 4 is on the dense set. Overall, persistence alone scores dense SS against the best reference, and we score .

19. Is the dashboard live?

Yes. Every model map comes from a fresh /simulate call to our server; nothing is pre-rendered. Kill the server and the dashboard shows "SERVER OFFLINE" and no model output. The scenario files only contain the 3 input days and the 10 observed days for comparison.

20. What did not work?

(a) The smoke model mostly failed "propagates": the shock decayed before it left the box. (b) A single model sometimes produced rain spikes above 5 std (stability failed on shocks). The ensemble fixed it. (c) The advection member was no clear win alone. (d) The monsoon-stall shock essentially never propagates ( of 72 windows). (e) Damping: damping every channel cost CSI and stopped the heavy-rain shock propagating; damping all but rain gained only +0.001 pooled and cost causality (−0.16), so it is not shipped. Retraining on all years cost causality too (−0.34). (f) Plans we dropped: rain in log space with softplus, a variance loss term, a learned climatology blend. The simpler design was enough.

21. Why does monsoon_stall fail to propagate?

Over 72 windows across seasons it propagates only of the time (warm_ocean: ). The response is localised and stable, so we still earn 3 of 4 quarters on it, but its energy outside box + 3 cells does not grow enough by days 4–8. Our reading: cutting the wind is a weak, fast-relaxing anomaly in an anomaly-space model, and the box sits on the grid's western edge. It is the most honest open problem we have.

22. How would you scale to 0.25°?

Same design, 4× more cells each way (≈ 128 × 144). Each cell is 4× smaller, so to allow the same physical speed per day the reach in cells must be 4× larger: either more layers or, better, 4 sub-steps per day each with its own small stencil, which keeps the causality guarantee. Convolutions scale linearly with cell count, about 16× compute per step, still feasible on GPU for training. We would need finer data and probably more channels.

23. What would you do with another week?

With more time: more ensemble members (which would also improve the risk probabilities) and seeds; log per-channel and per-lead scores; work on the stall propagation; add a slow ocean-memory channel; probabilistic rain; validate the impact rules against observed heat and rain outcomes.

24. Is it physics or machine learning?

Machine learning with physical structure built in: locality (finite speed), climatology anchoring, physical bounds, and an optional physically-motivated advection step with a CFL-like cap. It does not solve the equations of motion.

25. Couldn't the model just memorise dates?

It never receives a date, only day-of-year sin/cos as the contract specifies. The repo scan checks inference code for hardcoded dates and weather APIs, and for multi-year data archives. We ship a one-year climatology (365 days), which the rules allow.

26. How fast is it, and will it fit the judge's limits?

In a container mimicking the judge (offline, 4 CPU, 6 GB, read-only): per 10-step call (limit 5 s), ready in (limit 120 s), RAM (limit 6 GB). It also passes the kit's schema validator there.

27. What happens with bad input?

Wrong shapes or missing fields get a 422 error with a message, not a crash. NaN/Inf input cells (which the contract forbids) are filled from that day's climatology; malformed requests, including absurd step counts that overflow, return 422 (tested); any non-finite output falls back to climatology; rain and temperature are clamped, so output always passes the validity gate.

28. How does the model know about land, mountains and latitude?

Every step gets the land-sea mask, orography (in km), latitude and day-of-year as extra input channels, plus the climatology of the target day. They are bundled in the weights folder, since the API does not send them.

29. Your impact module says "heatwave". Is that IMD's definition?

No, and we say so on screen: we use daily-mean t2m ≥ 34 °C as a heat-stress proxy, because the data only has daily means; IMD heatwave criteria use daily maximum temperature. Dry day < 2.5 mm follows IMD's rainy-day threshold. It is a decision aid comparing Prime and Multiverse, not an official forecast.

30. A judge applies a new shock live, somewhere odd. What do we expect?

A response that starts in and around the box and spreads no faster than 6–8 cells per day; nothing changes beyond the reach ring. Its size depends on the channel and the season (a shock against the season may relax quickly). If it is applied at a map edge, the spread is one-sided. If the shock is zero, the output is identical.

31. How many parameters? How long to train?

Width 96, 5 blocks (4 for the advection members); about 7 minutes per run on an RTX 4050; the five weight files are about 1.6–2 MB each. (If asked for an exact count, train.py prints it at start-up.)

33. Did you try damping / retraining on all years? Why not shipped?

Yes, both, and we measured them. Damping shrinks long-lead anomalies towards climatology (out = clim + λ · (out − clim), λ per lead and variable, rain left alone). On 2021 it gained only +0.001 pooled and +0.002 per-lead skill, while over 72 windows it cost 0.16 points of causality (13.10 → 12.94). Retraining the same recipes on all years 2005–2021 left no held-out year, so any benefit is unmeasurable, and it cost 0.34 points of causality (13.10 → 12.76). So we ship ens5 exactly: 5 members trained on 2005–2019, rollouts averaged, no damping. Honest caveat: one sweep each over 72 correlated windows, so differences of about 0.3 may be partly noise; we chose the variant whose every number is measured out of sample.

34. Your demo years were in training, weren't they?

Mostly no. The shipped members were trained on 2005–2019 only, so the 2021 dashboard scenarios (Tauktae, Yaas, Kerala onset, Konkan rain, the January 2021 western disturbance) are genuinely unseen by the shipped model, and every held-out number is a number of the shipped model. The exception is the June 2019 heatwave scenario, which is a training year; we do not present it as a held-out forecast.

35. Does the warm-ocean shock always spread?

No. Over 72 windows across seasons (ens5, train/causality_sweep.py) it propagates in of them, with winter the weakest, and the stability check passes in . The monsoon_stall shock propagates in only . Mean causality over those windows is /15, and the null shock is identical in 72/72.

36. Are your probabilities calibrated?

For the dashboard risk maps, yes, and we measured it. The raw share of the 5 members above the threshold is over-confident (heavy-rain Brier skill −0.618 at day 4 and −0.923 at day 8, worse than normal odds). We blend it with the historical monthly frequency using per-lead weights fitted out of sample, and the calibrated probabilities beat normal odds on held-out 2021: heavy rain / / and heat / / at days 1 / 4 / 8. Honest limits: by day 8 heavy-rain skill is about zero (the map is nearly normal odds), the weights were fit on one year, and the risk maps are dashboard-only, not part of the judged score.

37. Why only 5 members?

Each member is a separate training run (7 to 15 minutes on one RTX 4050), and these 5 (3 plain, 2 wind-advection) are the set we selected and scored on held-out 2020-21. Five is enough to improve RMSE (ens5 beat every single member) but too few and too similar to give honest spread: the shares jump in steps of 0.2 and the members agree too often, which is why raw probabilities are over-confident and need calibration. More and more diverse members would help the probabilities most; that is first on our list for another week.
